Use AI to Prepare a Weekly Management Review

A weekly management review becomes useful when the people in the room can connect an operational question to the evidence needed for a decision. An AI assistant can help prepare that conversation by arranging notes, comparing definitions and drafting questions. It should not turn an incomplete scorecard into a confident explanation of performance. The preparation task is to make the state of the information clearer so that the responsible people can decide what to do next.

This guide expands the existing weekly-review resource on the books site. The methods, prompts and cases below are original editorial exercises, not quotations from Built to Run or claims about a particular chapter. Every team, label, result and meeting allocation in the exercise is fictional. No model has been run against these cases, and no client or company outcome is reported. The aim is to practice a bounded preparation workflow that keeps calculations, interpretations, proposals and decisions distinguishable.

Begin with the question the meeting must answer

A scorecard is not an agenda by itself. Before assembling one, write the management question in ordinary language. Are older requests waiting without a next step? Has a change in the work definition affected the reported completion count? Is a proposed action ready for someone to accept? Those questions require different evidence. A list of numbers headed performance can conceal those differences, especially when a person or assistant has combined updates written for unrelated purposes.

Choose a small number of questions that the group can actually discuss. State the decision expected from each: request missing information, select an investigation, accept a proposed action or defer a decision for a stated reason. Deferral can be a clear outcome when an essential definition is missing. It should not be disguised as a decision to improve the process. A useful agenda makes the unresolved condition visible enough that somebody knows what would allow the issue to return.

For a fictional service team, the central question could be whether to investigate a growing group of unfinished requests. That does not automatically become a decision to add staff, change a target or buy an AI tool. The review might first need the request population, the meaning of started, the snapshot time and the older items' next-step notes. The scope of the question helps determine which information belongs in the preparation packet and which speculation should be left out.

Give the meeting a stopping point. An investigation proposal can be considered complete when its question, owner, input and expected handoff are understood. That is different from resolving the underlying operational problem. Keeping the two stages separate prevents a weekly review from claiming progress simply because it assigned work. It also creates a more concrete starting point for the next review: did the promised investigation return the information needed to make the decision?

Prepare a small evidence packet before requesting an AI draft

For each measure, retain its name, definition, unit, population, period or snapshot, source and known limitations. The packet does not need to expose every underlying record to everybody. It needs enough context for the intended calculation and discussion, with access and sharing handled through the actual organization's rules. This exercise supplies only invented records. It does not authorize uploading private operating data to a model, and it makes no claim that a particular AI service is appropriate for those records.

Period measures and snapshot measures deserve separate labels. A count of items completed during a week is not the same thing as a count still open at one instant. An unfinished item's age is not its final completion duration. The Kanban Guide provides a limited primary reference for defining work and distinguishing flow measures. Its terminology can help clarify a discussion, but this article does not claim that the fictional team has implemented a Kanban system or that the supplied numbers support a delivery forecast.

Preserve corrections rather than silently replacing the earlier packet. A revised count should say which version it replaces and what changed in the counting basis. If a meeting note was based on version one, it should remain possible to explain why the next packet uses version two. The goal is not to build a large administrative register. A brief revision note can prevent an apparent trend from being explained as an operational change when it actually came from a changed definition or a corrected source.

Add a place for missing information. A blank field is not automatically zero, no problem or not applicable. In the fictional packet, an absent denominator remains absent until supplied. An assistant can draft a question about the gap, but it cannot infer the denominator from the tone of an update. Once missingness has a clear label, the meeting can distinguish a measure that reports an unfavorable result from a measure that cannot yet support the requested comparison.

Use AI to organize the packet without enlarging its claims

A bounded preparation instruction might say: use only the supplied fictional packet; preserve measure definitions, dates, units and source identifiers; separate stated facts, reproducible calculations, possible explanations and proposed actions; identify missing evidence; do not assign an action or mark it accepted unless the packet explicitly records acceptance. This is an original teaching prompt. It is not an observed model response, a company policy or evidence that a model will consistently follow every instruction.

Request a compact output with a question, relevant source rows, calculation if one is supported, unresolved condition and proposed discussion outcome. The source-row field matters because another person should be able to trace a drafted sentence back to the packet. A reference can still be wrong or irrelevant, so the presence of a source identifier is only a starting point for checking. Read the cited row and verify that it supports the precise statement, including its scope and any qualification.

Avoid asking the assistant to find the root cause from a small weekly scorecard. It can propose explanations as hypotheses when that is the requested task, but the output must preserve their status. A completion count may change because of demand, staffing, task size, a definition change or a source correction. The scorecard alone may not distinguish those explanations. An attractive narrative should not convert one possible cause into an established finding merely because it makes the meeting packet easier to read.

Keep the assistant's role separate from authority. Drafting an action sentence is not acceptance by the person named in it. Summarizing a proposed target is not approval of that target. A preparation workflow can remain useful while leaving those decisions to the responsible people. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework is a voluntary resource for thinking about AI use and evaluation; it is not a certification for this workflow or a substitute for the actual authority and context of an organization.

Work through six original weekly-review cases

Case A concerns an invented quality measure. The packet reports four reworked requests this week, compared with three in the earlier week, but gives no total number reviewed in either period. The count increased by one. A rework rate cannot be calculated from those fields. If an AI draft says quality declined by one third, it has moved from a count comparison to a broader interpretation without the relevant population. The meeting question is what denominators and consistent definitions are needed to compare rates.

The practical next step in A is deliberately small: request the reviewed population for both periods and confirm whether rework means the same event in each. Do not fill in a denominator from another chart just because it appears nearby. Once supplied, the new information might support a rate comparison, but it still would not by itself explain the cause. A disciplined review can acknowledge the extra reworked request now while leaving the percentage and causal interpretation unresolved.

Case B reports twelve completed requests under definition version one and nine under version two. The later version excludes requests that were closed and then reopened before the snapshot. The arithmetic difference is three, but it is not a comparison under a stable definition. The packet needs a reconciliation before the group labels the change a decline in delivery. An assistant can place the two definitions beside each other and draft the question; it should not choose which historical definition was correct.

A useful B discussion could request both periods on the same declared basis, if the source supports that reconstruction. If it does not, preserve the break in comparability. A chart with a clean line can be less informative than a note explaining the changed basis. The review does not need to erase the old measure to improve the current one. It needs to prevent a change in counting practice from being presented as a measured change in the underlying process.

Case C concerns action ownership. A note says that Mara could check the older requests by the next review. The packet contains no acceptance from Mara and no agreed handoff. An assistant may draft a proposed action for discussion, but it cannot report Mara's investigation as assigned or underway. The unresolved decision is whether the proposed owner accepts the scope and whether the expected output is clear enough to recognize when it arrives.

For C, a more precise proposal is to ask Mara whether she can review the five named fictional items and return the recorded next step or missing-next-step status for each. The proposed handoff is an information product, not a promise that the requests will be resolved. The actual owner and deadline remain subject to the group's decision. A sentence can become more concrete without falsely acquiring an approval that the source never recorded.

Case D reports that the number of open requests fell from twenty to fifteen. The later snapshot excludes a category included earlier. The five-item difference may partly or wholly reflect changed membership in the measured population. A preparation note should identify the category change before celebrating a reduction in outstanding work. The question is how many comparable items remained and whether the excluded category has been transferred, completed, withdrawn or simply omitted from this particular view.

The important distinction in D is between a smaller reported population and evidence of completed work. Neither a narrower view nor a transfer automatically establishes completion. Ask for the relevant source event or a comparable population before deciding what the reduction means. This is a focused example of scope checking, not a claim that every decrease in an open-item count is suspect. The packet itself supplies a specific reason why this comparison needs clarification.

Case E has four unfinished items with authored ages of two, five, eight and eleven days at an invented snapshot. Their mean unfinished age is 6.5 days, and the oldest is eleven days. Those are descriptions of that small unfinished population at that time. They are not an average completion duration, a promise that a new item will finish in 6.5 days or evidence that staffing is insufficient. The meeting can use the older items to ask about their next steps without pretending that the ages establish a forecast.

If one item finishes before the next review, the next snapshot contains a different population. Comparing the two means requires remembering that change in membership. A manager may still find the snapshot useful, but its meaning is narrower than a stable cohort study. The AI preparation task in E is to retain the word unfinished, the snapshot basis and the population count. A summary that drops those qualifiers can turn correct arithmetic into a misleading statement about delivery time.

Case F concerns a revision. An earlier packet says that eight requests were completed. A later correction says seven, because one record had been counted twice. The current count is seven, and the correction difference is negative one. The note does not establish that a request was lost, reopened or physically undone. An assistant should explain the revision as a counting correction supported by the packet, preserving the earlier version reference so that a reader can reconcile the meeting notes.

A useful F follow-up asks whether any previous decision depended on the eight-count figure. If so, revisit that decision with the corrected input. Do not automatically rerun every unrelated discussion or claim that the entire data source is unreliable. The correction has a bounded scope. The review should understand that scope, update the affected interpretation and retain a clear explanation of why the figures differ across two versions of the packet.

Turn preparation into a short decision conversation

An original forty-minute meeting allocation can reserve five minutes for previous commitments, ten for agreeing what the scorecard actually says, fifteen for one or two decision questions, five for accepting new actions and five for a read-back. Those blocks total forty minutes. They are a proposed teaching allocation, not a measured best practice or a promise that every management review fits that duration. Change the allocation when the actual purpose and participants require something else.

Begin with previous commitments using their promised handoffs. Ask whether the information or action returned, whether the owner accepted any revised scope and what remains unresolved. Completion of an administrative task is not necessarily improvement in an operating result. If somebody produced a requested list, record that delivery accurately. Whether the list supports a further decision is a separate question. This makes progress visible without inflating a completed preparation step into a verified business outcome.

During the scorecard discussion, distinguish an arithmetic check from agreement about meaning. The mean in E can be reproduced even while the group decides that another view would better support its question. Conversely, everybody can understand a proposed action while still lacking agreement on the owner. Keep those separate. A weekly review becomes harder to follow when one general approved label is used for correct numbers, accepted interpretations, authorized work and completed actions.

End with a read-back in plain language. Name the decision, accepted owner, expected handoff and unresolved condition if there is one. Invite a correction before the record is treated as settled. An AI assistant can draft the read-back from the actual notes afterward, but it should preserve dissent, qualifications and pending acceptance. A concise summary is valuable only if it leaves the decision in the state the participants actually reached.

Check the draft before using it as the meeting packet

Compare each consequential statement with the source packet. Look first for changed populations, missing denominators, causal words and action-status verbs. In the six fictional cases, the difference between could check and accepted ownership matters as much as an arithmetic result. A grammar pass cannot resolve that distinction. Ask whether a reader would come away believing something stronger than the underlying note, and revise the sentence if the answer is yes.

Use the downloadable CSV as a small practice set. It contains the six original cases, the tempting overstatement and a bounded discussion question. The examples are authored, not collected from real meetings. Arithmetic checks reproduce the stated differences, unfinished-age mean and proposed agenda total, while an editorial review examines the boundaries in the wording. Neither activity measures a model's performance because no model outputs have been supplied or scored in this exercise.

If you later evaluate an assistant, keep its actual prompt and response separate from the expected teaching examples. Define what counts as preserving scope before reviewing the responses, and retain cases that expose the errors relevant to your task. Do not rewrite the expected answer merely to make a polished response look successful. The evaluation should describe the tested task and limitations, rather than convert a small practice exercise into a general claim that the assistant is reliable for management decisions.

Questions about AI-assisted weekly management reviews

Should an AI assistant choose the measures for the business?

It can propose candidate measures and explain the information each would require. The responsible people still need to decide which questions matter and whether the definitions fit the workflow. A suggested metric is not an adopted policy. Start with the operational decision, identify the relevant population and source, and retain any unresolved measurement choice rather than accepting a scorecard solely because it looks complete.

Can we calculate a rate from a count alone?

A count can support a count comparison when the scope is understood. A rate also needs the relevant denominator and definition. Case A deliberately omits the reviewed population, so its four reworked requests do not establish a rework rate. Request the missing information and preserve the limitation in the draft. An assistant's estimate of an absent denominator does not turn that estimate into a source fact.

Does a lower open-item count prove better delivery?

Not by itself. Case D changes which category is included, so the smaller snapshot may reflect a narrower population. Check the membership and relevant completion events before interpreting the difference. Even a comparable decrease does not alone identify its cause. The meeting can request a specific reconciliation instead of treating a smaller displayed number as sufficient evidence for every favorable explanation.

When is a proposed action actually assigned?

For this exercise, assignment requires explicit acceptance by the responsible person and an understood handoff. A note saying somebody could investigate remains a proposal. An assistant should preserve that status until the record changes. Your actual organization may have its own authority and assignment rules; define those rather than assuming that a sentence generated in a preparation document creates a commitment or permission to act.

How should corrections appear in the next review?

Identify the current value, earlier version and reason for the correction, then revisit any affected interpretation or decision. Case F changes eight to seven because of a duplicate count, not because completed work was reversed. Preserve that distinction. A short, specific revision note is usually more useful for this exercise than either silently replacing the number or treating a bounded correction as proof that every other measure is invalid.

Does this guide claim to reproduce a book's management method?

No. The prompts, cases and meeting allocation are original editorial teaching material on a books-site resource page. They are not quotations, chapter summaries or verified descriptions of a manuscript. Use them to practice separating evidence from interpretation and proposals from decisions. Relevant books and other resources can be explored separately, with any claim about their actual text supported by the text itself.
