Read an Expected Value Before Asking AI to Recommend a Business Decision

Read an Expected Value Before Asking AI to Recommend a Business Decision

An expected value is easy to mistake for a promised result, especially when a reading note removes the table that produced it. A business book may describe uncertain outcomes, attach probabilities and summarize them with one number. Before asking an AI assistant to explain the conclusion, recover the quantity being averaged, the possible outcomes, their probabilities and the conditions under which the calculation applies. A correctly calculated average can still answer a different question from the reader's decision.

This guide develops an original reading workflow using invented finite examples. It does not quote a business book, attribute a method to an author or report an observed model response. No actual decision, client outcome, probability estimate or financial return has been evaluated. The examples use fictional planning units rather than money. Their arithmetic can be checked exactly, but that does not validate the assumptions that a real decision would require.

OpenStax's introductory treatment describes the expectation of a discrete random variable as a probability-weighted mean: multiply each possible value by its probability and sum the products. It discusses this as a long-run average rather than the outcome of a particular trial. That limited mathematical vocabulary is the source context for this guide. The teaching tables and reading instructions below are independently authored; no textbook worked example or business-book passage has been adopted.

Recover the variable before calculating its average

Begin by writing a sentence that names the random quantity. Is the passage averaging units completed, time consumed, a score or another outcome? Give the quantity a unit and a period if the supplied material provides them. If either is missing, preserve the gap. Calling everything value makes it difficult to notice when one table concerns gross output while another concerns net output after effort. A numerical comparison needs the quantities to match before their averages can meaningfully be compared.

In fictional case A, the declared quantity X can take zero, ten or twenty planning units with probabilities one-half, one-quarter and one-quarter. The probabilities sum to one. The weighted products are zero, five-halves and five, giving an expectation of fifteen-halves, or 7.5 units. None of the three possible outcomes is 7.5. The mean belongs to the distribution description; it need not be a value that any single occurrence can produce. This is a useful first check on the language of a summary.

An explanation that says the next occurrence will produce 7.5 units would add a prediction that the example does not support. An explanation that says 7.5 is the most likely outcome would also be wrong: zero has the largest supplied probability. Keep the mean, the most probable listed outcome and a single realized outcome distinct. They answer questions about different aspects of the same invented table. A reader can ask an assistant to name all three roles without asking it to make a real prediction.

Check the support of the variable as well as the formula. The support is the supplied set of possible values with positive probability. If a summary invents an additional outcome to make the average seem tangible, it has changed the problem. If it drops a zero-valued outcome because it contributes nothing to the sum, it may conceal a substantial part of the uncertainty. In case A, the zero product is arithmetically unremarkable but its one-half probability matters to the description of possible experience.

Check that the probability table is complete

A weighted sum is not automatically an expectation for the full situation. The listed possibilities must be the intended outcomes, and their probabilities must form the declared distribution. A list of favorable scenarios may leave out unsuccessful or unobserved possibilities. A set of rounded percentages may also require clarification. Do not silently normalize an incomplete table merely to produce a clean number. That transformation changes the probability assumptions and can conceal the reason the full expectation remains unresolved.

Fictional case B lists outcomes ten and twenty with probabilities one-quarter each. Their probability mass totals one-half. The known contribution is five-halves plus five, or fifteen-halves. The other half is unspecified. If the missing outcome were zero, the full expectation would be 7.5. If it were thirty, the full expectation would be 22.5. These are explicit hypothetical completions, not inferred facts. The supplied partial table alone does not identify a single full expectation.

Renormalizing the two known probabilities to one-half each gives fifteen units. That is the mean of a different, explicitly conditioned or reweighted description. It cannot be presented as the original full expectation unless the relevant relationship is actually established. The useful reading note therefore contains two statements: the known weighted contribution is 7.5 units, and the full expectation is unresolved because half the probability mass lacks an outcome value. The second statement prevents the first from being mistaken for a complete answer.

Ask a precise follow-up question of the material: does the author intend the table to cover every possible outcome, only selected scenarios or a conditional subset? A chapter heading, table note or surrounding paragraph may answer it, but this guide has not inspected such material from any actual business book. When the relevant passage is absent, an assistant should identify the missing context. It should not supply the omitted probability mass from a familiar story about business success or failure.

Compare distributions that share a mean

Fictional case C compares two declared alternatives. Alternative S always produces ten planning units. Alternative T produces zero or twenty with equal probability. Both expectations equal ten, yet their supplied possibilities differ. S has one listed outcome; T has two, and zero occurs with probability one-half. Equal means do not make these distributions identical, and the arithmetic does not choose between them for a real person. Constraints, preferences and the validity of the underlying assumptions remain separate questions.

Use the shared mean as the start of a comparison rather than its conclusion. Ask what the possible outcomes are, which lower outcomes are present and which conditions matter to the reader's question. If the task requires at least five units on a particular occurrence, the supplied fictional tables give S probability one and T probability one-half of meeting that threshold. That is a finite membership calculation, not an assessment of actual reliability or an operating recommendation.

A reading summary can preserve the distinction in two sentences: the alternatives have the same supplied expected quantity, while their possible outcomes and threshold probabilities differ. This is clearer than adding a vague risk label without defining what risk means. It also avoids treating a preference as a mathematical theorem. An assistant may organize the comparison, but a statement that one alternative is universally better would require criteria beyond the mean and beyond the invented table.

Apply a transformation to outcomes before averaging

A passage may move from an average quantity to a score derived from that quantity. Read the order of operations carefully. Transforming each possible outcome and then averaging generally answers a different question from transforming the mean. A summary that replaces the full table with its average can lose the information needed for the first calculation. The problem is particularly easy to miss when both expressions contain the same numbers and the same familiar word, expected.

Fictional case D uses outcomes zero and twenty planning units, each with probability one-half. Its mean is ten units. Define a separate teaching score as the square of the outcome's numerical value, measured in squared planning units. The possible scores are zero and four hundred, so their expected score is two hundred. Squaring the mean gives one hundred. The difference is one hundred squared units. These two operations produce different answers even though they start from the same complete probability table.

The square is an invented mathematical transformation, not a claim that a real reader should value larger outcomes quadratically. Its purpose is to expose an invalid substitution. If an actual passage defines a nonlinear utility, penalty or score, the reader needs that definition and the individual outcomes. An assistant should retain the expression being averaged and show the products. It should not introduce a utility function merely because the passage contains uncertainty or because a particular decision theory sounds relevant.

Keep the transformed unit visible. Ten planning units and two hundred squared planning units are different quantities. A comparison that places them in one unlabeled column is not repaired by more decimal places. Write the input quantity, transformation and resulting quantity as three separate phrases. If the transformation has no stated interpretation, call it a mathematical teaching score and leave its practical meaning unresolved. This makes the calculation inspectable without pretending it supplies a decision criterion.

Separate gross outcomes from a fixed deduction

Fictional case E returns to outcomes zero and twenty planning units with equal probability. Their expected gross quantity is ten. Suppose the example explicitly deducts three planning units on every occurrence, regardless of which outcome occurs. The possible net outcomes become negative three and seventeen, each with probability one-half. Their weighted sum is seven units. Subtracting the fixed three from the gross mean also gives seven. The deduction is part of the invented definition, not an observed expense or a proposed business policy.

This agreement depends on the specified fixed deduction. If the deduction varies with the outcome, the reader needs that relationship or the corresponding complete net table. A summary should not subtract the most favorable listed cost from the gross average while leaving other cases unmentioned. Nor should it silently treat an unknown cost as zero. The useful question is whether the variable whose expectation is being reported already includes the deduction. Subtracting it again would change the defined quantity a second time.

The example also provides a controlled unit check. Define sixty small planning units as one large planning unit. The same expected net quantity is seven large units or four hundred twenty small units. The larger numeral does not represent a larger outcome. A reading note should carry the conversion rule and the unit through each comparison. This is particularly helpful when an assistant combines a table with prose that uses a different scale, because the numerical discrepancy might be a representation change rather than a substantive disagreement.

Preserve negative outcomes when they are permitted by the declared variable. Replacing negative three with zero would truncate the net distribution and change its expectation to eight and one-half. That might describe a different rule, but the original table did not authorize it. An assistant asked to simplify the explanation should shorten the wording without changing the support. A short explanation can still say that a fixed deduction makes one possible net outcome negative and that the average remains seven.

Make sensitivity assumptions explicit

Fictional case F assigns outcome negative four probability one minus p and outcome sixteen probability p. The parameter p must lie between zero and one, but its value has not initially been supplied. Multiplying and adding gives the symbolic expectation negative four plus twenty times p. This expression is a conditional calculation, not a probability estimate. Without a specified p, the example has no single numerical expectation. Familiarity with a business story does not establish the missing parameter.

At a hypothetical p of one-quarter, the expectation is one planning unit. At a hypothetical p of one-half, it is six. The change is five units because the probability parameter changed by one-quarter and the outcome gap is twenty. These are two explicitly chosen scenarios. They are not a forecast range, confidence interval or evidence that either probability is plausible. A reader can use the comparison to see which assumption drives the arithmetic while leaving the actual likelihood unresolved.

The symbolic mean equals zero when p equals one-fifth. It is positive for p above one-fifth and negative below it. This is a boundary for the declared mean, not a universal approval threshold. A real task could depend on a minimum individual outcome, available capacity or consequences omitted from the variable. Even within this invented table, knowing the sign of the mean does not remove the negative outcome. The probability of that outcome remains one minus p whenever it is positive.

Ask an assistant to distinguish supplied inputs from chosen sensitivity values. A useful instruction is to retain p as unknown in the main answer, then show the two labeled hypothetical substitutions separately. The assistant can explain how the expression changes without assigning a likelihood. If the source supplies a probability later, record its location and conditions before substitution. Do not let the hypothetical number migrate into a subsequent summary as if it had been reported by the author.

Use AI to reconstruct the calculation without outsourcing the assumptions

A bounded prompt can ask for five concrete outputs: the random quantity with its unit, the possible outcomes, their stated probabilities, the weighted products and any missing context. Provide the relevant passage and table together when you have permission to use them. Tell the assistant to separate direct statements from its own explanatory wording. Request that unresolved probabilities remain unresolved. This is a proposed reading exercise; no assistant response to this prompt has been observed or evaluated here.

Follow with a comparison task that asks whether the prose conclusion matches the calculated quantity. In case A, the calculation supports a mean of 7.5 but not a next outcome of 7.5. In case B, it supports a known contribution but not a full mean. In case C, equal means coexist with different threshold probabilities. These distinct failures cannot be repaired by checking multiplication alone. The reading task includes preserving what the number describes and the limits of the supplied evidence.

Keep a compact correction alongside the relevant sentence rather than replacing the whole passage with a general caution about uncertainty. For example, change a claim that the average is the most likely result into a sentence that names the mean and separately identifies the modal listed outcome. If the source does not establish a full distribution, state precisely what is missing. Specific corrections help the reader compare the original claim with the proposed summary and avoid vague language that makes every numerical statement seem equally doubtful.

An independent arithmetic check should read the same authored inputs that appear in the worksheet. Exact fractions are useful for these finite teaching cases because they keep one-quarter and one-half exact throughout the calculations. Such a check can establish consistency between the declared inputs and the stated sums. It cannot authenticate a book passage, validate an actual probability estimate or establish that a decision objective is appropriate. The distinction belongs in the explanation of the check, not only in a footnote after the conclusion.

Keep the reading conclusion proportional to the evidence

Finish with a sentence that states what the reconstructed number establishes. Name the variable, the supplied distribution and the result, then identify the remaining question. For an incomplete table, report the known contribution and the unknown part. For equal means, preserve the differing possible outcomes. For a transformed score, name the transformation. For a sensitivity exercise, retain the conditional parameter. This produces a usable reading note without converting mathematical consistency into a recommendation.

When comparing chapters or editions, do not assume that a reused symbol refers to the same quantity. One passage may use an average per occurrence while another uses a total over a period, or may introduce a deduction only later. Verify the definitions before combining the numbers. If the needed passage is unavailable in the material supplied for the task, record that limit. This guide has not inspected an actual manuscript or business-book example and does not attribute these fictional exercises to any author.

Frequently asked questions

Can an expected value be a number that never occurs?

Yes. In case A, only zero, ten and twenty are possible, while the weighted mean is 7.5. That mean summarizes the declared distribution. It does not add a fourth possible outcome, identify the most likely listed result or guarantee what the next occurrence produces. Preserve those separate meanings when shortening a reading note.

Should I normalize probabilities that add to less than one?

First determine why the table is incomplete. Case B has one-half of its mass unspecified, so the full expectation remains unresolved. Renormalizing the listed outcomes produces fifteen, which answers a different reweighted or conditional question. Show that transformation only with an explicit label and a reason supported by the supplied context.

Do equal expected values make two alternatives equivalent?

They establish equality of that one summary quantity under the supplied assumptions. Case C has equal means but different possible outcomes and different probabilities of meeting the declared five-unit threshold. A real comparison requires its own criteria and evidence. The example does not recommend an alternative or validate a decision rule.

Can I square the mean to obtain the expected squared outcome?

Not in general. Case D gives a mean of ten, a squared mean of one hundred and an expected squared outcome of two hundred. Apply the specified transformation to each outcome before weighting when that is the requested quantity. Keep squared units and the original units distinct, and do not invent a practical interpretation for the score.

Does a fixed deduction change every outcome or only the mean?

In case E, the declared deduction applies to every occurrence. Zero and twenty become negative three and seventeen, and the mean becomes seven. The same fixed subtraction works on the mean, but that agreement does not authorize substituting an unknown or outcome-dependent deduction. Check whether the source already reports a net quantity before subtracting again.

What can I conclude when a probability is unknown?

You can preserve a symbolic expression and show clearly labeled hypothetical substitutions. Case F yields negative four plus twenty times p; choosing one-quarter gives one and choosing one-half gives six. Neither substitution estimates p. The zero-mean boundary at one-fifth is arithmetic within the invented table, not an actual operating recommendation or evidence that any probability is correct.
