Use a Business Book Index to Find Passages Before an AI Summary

A business book index can help you find a passage worth reading, but it cannot supply the passage's argument on its own. A heading and a page number identify a place to investigate. They do not tell you whether the author supports an idea, criticizes it, gives an exception or merely introduces someone else's view. Before asking AI to summarize what a book says about a topic, follow the index to material you can actually inspect.

This guide presents an original reading workflow with six fictional index examples. The headings, page labels, edition labels and reading states were authored for teaching. None comes from an actual book index, manuscript, quotation or observed model output. The downloadable worksheet makes navigation distinctions concrete. Local checks verify the supplied relationships and arithmetic, not interpretation accuracy or reader performance. No claim about a particular author's method or endorsement is made.

Begin with a question instead of an isolated keyword

Write what you want to understand in one sentence. A question about why a handoff becomes delayed is more useful than the single word handoff because it tells you what kind of passage you need. You may be looking for a definition, a comparison, a proposed explanation or a practical example. Those are different reading tasks, even if an index places them under neighboring headings. Keep the question visible while choosing which entries to follow.

Do not require the book to use your preferred wording. Your question may use a workplace expression while the index uses another term or a more specific subheading. Start with the words you have, then inspect nearby entries and explicit cross-references. A candidate term is a navigation lead, not proof that two concepts have the same meaning. The existing glossary guide explains how to keep tentative meanings tied to the source context where they were encountered.

The primary-source context here is deliberately limited. UCL Press describes main entries, more specific subentries, locators and cross-references, and distinguishes an index from a list of every word occurrence. Hopkins Press likewise distinguishes an index from an outline or commentary and emphasizes useful cross-references. These publishing guidelines help name the navigation parts. They do not establish the conventions of an unseen book, certify an index or demonstrate that an AI can infer a book's content from headings.

The workflow below is for reading an existing index. It does not generate a publishable index from a manuscript. Selecting entries, establishing locators and evaluating a complete index would require the actual text and a different task. Here, the reader uses a supplied navigation aid to choose material for inspection, then separates what was found from what remains unresolved. That boundary makes the later AI request easier to review.

Case A: a cross-reference is a direction to another entry

Case A authors the entry handoffs with a See cross-reference to transfers. The supplied transfers entry has locators 12 and 48. The handoffs entry itself has no locators in this invented index. Following the cross-reference leads to the transfers entry and its two locators. It does not establish that a passage exists at the cross-reference line itself, that the two terms are interchangeable in every context or that either passage answers the reader's question.

A useful reading note records the route taken: initial question, starting heading, cross-reference target and locators to inspect. That preserves why the reader reached the pages without turning the navigation wording into a claim about the author. If a model later drafts a summary, it can distinguish the route used to find the material from the actual inspected content. Do not ask it to fabricate a definition from the fact that one heading redirects to another.

The example uses a declared convention. In an actual book, read the index's own instructions or typography where available before assuming that every mark has the same role. A See direction and a See also direction should not be flattened into the same relationship without checking the supplied structure. They may lead the reader through different routes. The point is to retain the exact navigation relationship, not to impose a universal index-editing rule on an unseen edition.

If the target entry is absent from a supplied excerpt, stop the claim at that boundary. You can report that the excerpt points to transfers, but not which pages the unseen entry contains. A model may suggest asking for the target entry; it should not fill in plausible locators. The authored worksheet includes the target so the local check can reproduce the route. That completeness belongs to this teaching case, not to every index fragment a reader might paste into a task.

Case B: keep subheadings attached to their parent topic

Case B supplies a main entry queues with two subentries: waiting has locator 14 and capacity has locator 39. The complete navigation descriptions are queues, waiting and queues, capacity. Copying waiting, 14 into a note without its parent can make it look like a different independent topic. Keep the hierarchy visible when asking AI to organize candidate passages, especially when several main entries contain similar subheading words.

The two locators do not establish the relationship between waiting and capacity in the book's argument. Perhaps the passages define terms, compare situations or discuss separate examples. Their content is not supplied in this fictional case. A reader should inspect the relevant material before writing a causal or practical conclusion. The index hierarchy helps choose where to look; it does not replace the reasoning that would be needed to connect what the passages say.

A useful comparison note has room for the parent entry, subheading, locator and current reading state. The reading state might be not yet inspected, inspected but unclear or ready for a bounded own-word note. These are original workflow labels, not a publisher's annotation system. They help prevent a list of promising locators from being misreported later as a set of passages that have already been read and understood.

If the original index uses indentation, a copied text excerpt may lose that visual relationship. Before drawing conclusions, establish whether a line is a subentry or another main entry. Do not let an assistant guess the structure from a bare word and number when the layout context is missing. The linked Elixir guide to preparing PDF text discusses why extracted text can lose reading order and other context. It does not inspect the actual book for this guide.

Case C: a page range differs from separate page locators

Case C authors two alternative entries for an invented topic, buffer review. Version R supplies the inclusive span 40–43. Version S supplies the separate locators 40 and 43. The stated inclusive span contains four integer page labels: 40, 41, 42 and 43. The separate locator list contains two labels. These counts describe the supplied navigation expressions; they do not show how many relevant paragraphs or substantive discussions occur on the pages.

Do not turn the range into a claim that every sentence on all four pages discusses the topic. Likewise, do not assume that pages 41 and 42 are irrelevant because the alternative list names only 40 and 43. The examples supply navigation forms, not page content. The reader must inspect the actual material to understand the extent and nature of a discussion. Counting page labels is a helpful check on a plan, not an assessment of argument coverage.

Preserve the original locator spelling in your notes before expanding a range for planning purposes. Some indexes use shortened numbers or special notation for notes, illustrations or tables. This guide does not interpret every possible convention. In Case C the endpoints are explicitly written in full and the inclusive interpretation is declared. If a real expression is ambiguous, ask for the index's convention or the surrounding entry instead of silently guessing an expansion.

When you request a summary, state which pages were actually supplied or inspected. If you read only the first page of a listed span, that can still support a limited note about that page. It cannot support a claim about the entire range. A model should not turn an index locator into evidence of complete access. Keep the reading boundary visible even when the navigation suggests that more relevant material may follow.

Case D: an edition label belongs with its locators

Case D invents two editions of a fictional book called Harbor Work Notes. In edition EX-E1, its supplied index points lead time to page 27. In edition EX-E2, the supplied index points lead time to page 35, while page 27 is labeled another topic in the authored mapping. This is an invented mismatch designed to show why a locator needs an edition context. It is not an inspection of real editions or a finding that a published index is wrong.

If two readers are discussing a book, first establish which edition or format each is using. A page number can be precise within one supplied version and unhelpful in another. Use the source information actually available to distinguish the versions rather than asking a model to invent publication details. If the relationship between the versions is unresolved, keep that unresolved state in the discussion note instead of claiming that everyone read the same passage.

A topic heading can provide a useful lead across versions, but it does not prove that the wording, argument or examples remained unchanged. The reader needs the relevant material from each version to compare those claims. This guide does not recommend inferring a revision history from a changed page number. Layout, inserted content or other differences could matter in a real case; none was investigated here. Only the authored locator mappings are available.

For a shared reading session, record a location description appropriate to the actual copy: the edition information you can verify, the relevant heading and the locator supplied by that version. Do not treat an ebook position or a PDF image number as an automatic substitute for a printed page. The existing footnote source-chain guide discusses coordinate questions separately. The practical goal here is to keep the index route attached to the copy in which it was found.

Case E: an index concept is not an exact-word search result

Case E supplies the fictional index heading buffers with locator 18. It also supplies an original teaching description for page 18: a waiting reserve is discussed, while the literal character sequence buffer does not occur in that supplied description. A case-insensitive exact substring search for buffer returns no match in that one description. The index still supplies its authored navigation lead. This does not establish the content of a real page or evaluate a search engine.

The distinction helps when a reader searches an extracted file and concludes that a topic is absent because one preferred word was not found. An exact-word search and a curated topic index answer different navigation questions. The supplied description in Case E is intentionally limited. Its missing literal word does not prove the concept absent from an unseen full page, and the index heading does not independently prove how the concept is explained there.

Use AI to suggest candidate search terms only if they remain clearly labeled candidates. A proposed synonym is a way to look for material, not an established statement that the book uses the terms equivalently. Keep a record of which term came from the index, which came from your own question and which was an optional AI suggestion. That makes it easier to return to the actual wording when a later explanation becomes too broad.

Do not describe a search failure as a complete reading result. Perhaps the supplied extraction omitted a caption, a table or an entire section; perhaps the relevant concept uses different language. Those are questions to investigate rather than diagnoses of the file. The existing PDF-text guide addresses extraction scope. This guide adds the reading distinction: an index lead can direct inspection while neither a word match nor its absence settles the passage's meaning.

Case F: keep unread locators out of an evidence summary

Case F supplies a fictional entry delegation with locators 21 and 99. The reader's own note says page 21 was inspected but page 99 was not supplied or inspected. There are two listed locators and one inspected locator in the authored record. The fraction one-half is a locator-selection count only. It does not measure semantic coverage, comprehension or the proportion of the book's argument that has been verified.

A bounded summary may discuss the reader's authorized own-word note about page 21, provided it retains that limited scope. It should not claim to summarize the book's complete treatment of delegation. Page 99 might add a condition, another example or a different discussion; its content is unknown here. Avoid even choosing among those possibilities as if one had been observed. The right next question is whether further reading is needed for the claim the reader wants to make.

A navigation worksheet can separate listed, supplied and inspected. A page can be listed in an index without being supplied to a model, and it can be supplied without having been carefully inspected by the person making a claim. These original state labels help the reader describe the actual workflow. They do not provide an automatic measure of reading quality. The local check verifies the authored states, not whether an actual person understood a real passage.

If the task requires both locators, leave the whole-topic summary pending until the required material is available. If the task needs only the passage at 21, narrow the wording accordingly. A useful AI request makes that choice explicit rather than asking the assistant to make a complete answer from incomplete evidence. The model can help organize unresolved questions, but it cannot convert an unread locator into inspected content by producing plausible prose.

Turn the navigation route into a bounded AI request

After following an index lead, write a short source note in your own words. Keep the initial question, index heading and hierarchy, edition context, actual locator and reading state together. Add the particular claim or question you want help understanding. This keeps the later task grounded in what you actually inspected rather than in a list of headings that merely looked promising. It also makes it easier to detect a summary that overstates the source scope.

An original request might say: organize these supplied own-word notes about one inspected passage; preserve the entry and subentry labels as navigation context; distinguish observations from my questions; do not infer the contents of other listed pages. This is a proposed teaching instruction, not a tested prompt or an observed model response. A future response would need review against the actual authorized notes and source, particularly before attributing a claim to an author.

Do not ask the model to recreate a missing book passage or produce an invented quotation from the index. If a quotation matters, use the exact passage you are authorized to inspect and verify its wording and attribution directly. This guide contains no actual book quotations. The six examples are original navigation cases, and their fictional labels should never be reused in a real citation as if they identified published material.

A useful result might be a clearer list of questions: whether two inspected passages use a term consistently, whether a stated condition applies to your example, or which source detail is still missing. That is different from a claim that the author recommends a particular operating decision. Keep the existing argument-mapping and decision-scenario owners for those later tasks. The index workflow finds material to read; it does not supply a book-derived business method by itself.

Choose a stopping point that matches the reading purpose

You do not need to exhaust every related entry to write a small, accurately scoped note. You do need enough inspected material for the claim you intend to make. If the goal is to understand one definition, a bounded passage may be appropriate. If the goal is to compare the book's treatment of a topic across chapters, the relevant scope may be larger. State the purpose first so that a partial reading is not accidentally presented as a comprehensive one.

When an index route fails to answer the question, return to the question before expanding the search endlessly. Perhaps the book addresses another issue, the supplied index excerpt is incomplete or a different term is needed. Keep the outcome useful by recording what was actually checked and why the question remains open. A navigation dead end is information about this reading attempt, not proof that the author never discusses the subject anywhere.

For a reading group, share unresolved navigation questions before sharing confident conclusions. Another reader may have a different edition, a more complete index or a relevant passage already inspected. Compare those sources explicitly. Do not combine several people's page labels into an apparent consensus about unseen content. The practical benefit is traceability: each contribution can remain attached to the material and version that actually supported it.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI summarize a topic from index entries alone?

An index can identify candidate locations, but it does not supply the passages' claims or qualifications. Use it to choose material to inspect, then ground any summary in the authorized material actually available. Do not infer an author's position, quotation or operating method from a heading and locator alone.

Is a cross-reference another passage locator?

Not in the way Case A is authored. Its starting entry directs the reader to another entry, whose locators are supplied separately. Preserve that navigation relationship and check the actual book's convention. If the target entry is missing from an excerpt, leave its unseen locators unresolved.

Does a page range show how much of the argument I read?

No. Case C's four integer page labels describe an explicitly inclusive navigation span. They do not count relevant paragraphs or measure comprehension. Record the material actually inspected and keep any summary limited to that scope instead of treating the range as proof of complete access.

Can two editions use the same page number differently?

Case D deliberately authors that situation. Its edition labels and mappings are fictional, not a real revision comparison. For an actual discussion, identify each copy and inspect the relevant material before claiming that different readers reached the same passage or that a changed locator proves a changed argument.

Does a failed word search show the index is wrong?

Case E supports no such conclusion. Its exact search examines one supplied teaching description, while the index provides a separate topic lead. The actual page content and search scope would need inspection before diagnosing a real discrepancy. Candidate synonyms should remain suggestions until the source establishes their meaning.

What do the downloadable cases prove?

They reproduce authored cross-reference targets, hierarchy labels, locator counts, date-independent edition mappings, literal word-search behavior and stated reading boundaries. They do not verify an actual index, book argument, manuscript, quotation, model response, reader outcome, professional review or author endorsement. Their purpose is to make the next reading question precise.
