Operating System Mistakes: Test One Handoff Before Scaling It

Use a fictional quote-request handoff to define acceptance, count unique work, test exceptions and maintain clear operating instructions.

Published 2026-09-23Updated 2026-11-21Robertson Books educational guide

A business operating system becomes useful when people can repeat a handoff, recognize an exception and show that the work reached its intended finish. A document library or a new application can support that process, but neither establishes that it works. Start with one recurring handoff and test the actual instructions.

This is an original Robertson Books website exercise for business owners. It is not a manuscript excerpt, a summary of a verified book chapter or a report of client results. The examples and records below are fictional.

Mistake 1: defining an activity without a finish

“Send the quote request to operations” describes an action. It does not say when operations has enough information to prepare the quote or who handles an incomplete request. Define the start, receiving role and observable finish before choosing a form or application.

For this fictional exercise, the start is a salesperson recording a customer request. The receiving role is a quote coordinator. The handoff is accepted only when the requested work, service location, timing constraint and source reference are present, and the coordinator has acknowledged ownership. Acceptance means the request is ready for the next step; it does not mean a price has been approved or a quote delivered.

Mistake 2: testing only whether the sender pressed send

Ask someone in the receiving role to use the instructions on a sample record. Observe what they must ask, infer or retrieve elsewhere. If the sender marks a task complete while the receiver cannot act on it, the system has measured transmission rather than a usable handoff.

Use a short acceptance check: identify the record, confirm the required facts, name the next owner and state what remains unresolved. Let the receiver reject an incomplete handoff to a defined exception queue. A rejection should identify the missing item and the person responsible for supplying it, rather than silently returning the work to a general inbox.

Mistake 3: counting messages as unique work

The five invented records below concern four unique requests. H-04 repeats H-01. H-05 was withdrawn before work began. Keep both facts visible so the working queue is not inflated by a duplicate or confused with closed history.

Mistake 4: demonstrating only the successful path

Test the instructions with a missing scope, duplicate submission, changed deadline and unavailable coordinator. For each case, ask where the record goes, who is notified and what lets the work resume. Do not let an automation silently assign a substitute deadline or manufacture an acceptance.

Use a stopping rule for the trial: do not expand it until the receiving role can locate the instructions and handle the tested exceptions without guessing about authority. That is a proposed acceptance rule for your process review, not proof that every future case is covered. Document what remains outside the trial.

Mistake 5: assigning a task without decision authority

The coordinator may own preparation while a manager approves pricing. The salesperson may obtain missing information without being authorized to promise a delivery date. Write those boundaries down. A single owner can coordinate several contributors without having authority over every decision.

Identify a backup role, how an absence is communicated and which decisions require escalation. If the backup cannot access the necessary record, the backup arrangement is incomplete. Test access and handoff responsibility using approved sample information rather than sensitive customer data.

Mistake 6: changing the process without maintaining its record

Name the instruction owner and version. Record why a required field, acceptance check or approval role changes. Tell the people affected which version applies, and keep earlier decision references when they explain work already performed. Several unmarked copies create uncertainty even when each copy reads clearly.

At the next operating review, inspect accepted and blocked records against the definitions. Ask whether the same missing item recurs and whether staff are bypassing a step that is difficult to use. Revise one specific part, test it and retain the result. A completion percentage alone cannot explain the cause of rework.

Run one bounded handoff test

Choose a recurring transfer of work and write its trigger, required evidence, receiving owner, acceptance condition and exception route. Try the normal case and at least one incomplete case with the people who actually perform the roles. Record the result, unresolved limits and the next review owner.
