Real Estate Ownership Mistakes: Trace Both Sides of the Rent

Trace three fictional business-and-property cash cases, avoid double-counting rent, and separate entity cash shortages from a combined remainder. Includes CSV.

Published 2026-09-23Updated 2026-11-21Robertson Books educational guide

Buying the building and operating the business create different questions. A rent payment can support the property while reducing the operating company’s available cash. Keep both sides visible before describing either business as strong enough to support an ownership decision.

This guide uses three original fictional cash cases. No actual property, business, lease, book passage or client result was examined. The arithmetic is an educational reconciliation, not financial statements, a valuation, tax treatment, a financing approval or a recommendation to buy rather than lease.

Mistake 1: leaving the cash boundary undefined

Give each record a name and a period. In this illustration, one owner controls both the operating business and the property business. The operating business uses the building and transfers rent to the property business. All figures describe one simplified month, with the transfer received in that same month.

The operating business starts with $14,000 after its other assumed operating cash outflows but before rent. The property receives $6,000 of rent, pays $1,800 of other property cash costs and pays $3,200 of principal and interest. These are authored inputs, not evidence of market rent, actual expenses or available financing.

The SBA’s business-management guidance describes buying and leasing business assets, including leasing commercial space. That establishes the existence of different arrangements, not which is appropriate for this fictional owner. The comparison here asks a narrower question: where does the same cash go?

Mistake 2: counting rent as new cash for the same owner

After the $6,000 transfer, the operating business retains $8,000. The property retains $1,000 after $1,800 of other costs and $3,200 of principal and interest. Adding the two remainders gives $9,000. The rent is already an outflow on one side and an inflow on the other; adding it again would double count the transfer.

Check the same boundary from outside: $14,000 before rent, less $1,800 paid outside the pair for property costs, less $3,200 paid for debt service, leaves $9,000. This is a cash cross-check for the declared same-owner illustration. It is not a consolidation procedure, and it does not establish that either entity can distribute its remaining cash.

Mistake 3: treating an internal adjustment as a cure

The second row changes only operating cash before rent, from $14,000 to $10,000. The operating remainder falls to $4,000, while the property still retains $1,000. Combined remaining cash is $5,000. Nothing about owning both businesses prevents that decline.

The third row illustrates a different allocation of the same $10,000 starting cash. If only $4,000 is transferred, the operating business retains $6,000 but the property is short $1,000: $4,000 − $1,800 − $3,200. The combined figure remains $5,000. Moving cash between the two records does not create more cash at the outside boundary.

This hypothetical smaller transfer is not permission to reduce a contractual payment. The illustration establishes no right to change rent, delay debt service or move money between entities. Keep the actual agreements, restrictions, payment dates and qualified advice separate from a spreadsheet scenario. A positive combined number can coexist with a cash shortage in one entity.

Mistake 4: mixing a cash remainder with profit or return

The $3,200 line includes assumed principal and interest. The other $1,800 groups assumed property costs. No escrow payment is embedded in the debt line in this example. In a real file, reconcile the payment breakdown and each included expense so the same cost is neither omitted nor counted twice.

The remainders exclude acquisition funding, closing costs, capital replacements, additional reserve contributions, income taxes and distributions. They are not net operating income, accounting profit, taxable income, cash available to distribute or a total investment return. A result can be arithmetically correct and still omit a material cash requirement.

Separate an expense estimate from an actual paid amount, and distinguish money available now from a projected receipt. Keep restricted or third-party funds outside the owner’s available-cash calculation. When a payment crosses the month boundary, reconcile the date instead of assuming that both records recognized the same transfer at the same time.

Mistake 5: relying on one document to answer every question

Prepare an evidence list for the specific proposal. Identify the parties, property, period, intended use, governing agreements, loan payment breakdown, insurance information, condition findings and capital scope. Ask what each record actually establishes and which question remains unanswered.

An operating projection does not establish title, permitted use, property condition or lender acceptance. A payment record does not prove that every obligation has been satisfied. Obtain the relevant source and appropriate professional review for the actual transaction; this fictional cash exercise cannot resolve those matters.

Give each missing item an accountable role and review date. If the property arrangement, operating forecast or financing changes, preserve the prior version and explain the change. A later reviewer should be able to reproduce the figures without reconstructing an undocumented conversation.

Use a reading note to improve the next question

When reading an ownership book, preserve the passage locator and distinguish the author’s stated claim from your proposed application. Write the question the reading raises, identify the actual record that could answer it, and keep that record separate from the reading note. Do not present an invented illustration as a quoted chapter method.
