What to Do With Everyone Who Says No

By Dr. Connor Robertson

Photo via Unsplash

Every conversation I have about prospecting eventually arrives at the same complaint. Someone tells me they made forty calls last week, had six real conversations, and got one meeting. Then they ask me what they should be saying differently on the call.

It is a fair question, and the answer usually involves something they could tighten in the opening. But it is not the question that would change their results the most. The bigger opportunity is sitting in the thirty-nine conversations they already wrote off.

The Number Nobody Tracks

Most people who prospect track two numbers: calls made and meetings set. Everything in between disappears. The call either converted or it did not, and the ones that did not get mentally filed under "not interested" and forgotten.

That filing system is where most of the value leaks out. "Not interested" is a category that contains at least four completely different situations, and treating them identically is why so much prospecting work produces so little compounding return.

The first situation is a genuine mismatch. The person you called does not have the problem you solve, will not have it later, and there is nothing to build on. This is the only group that actually deserves to be discarded, and in my experience it is a much smaller share of calls than people assume.

The second is a timing problem. The need is real, but the moment is wrong. They just signed with someone else, they are mid-renovation, they are three weeks from a closing, the budget cycle resets in October. Nothing about the fit is wrong. The calendar is wrong.

The third is an authority problem. You reached someone who cannot say yes and therefore said no, which is the safest answer available to a person without decision rights. Their no was accurate about their own authority and irrelevant to the opportunity.

The fourth is a trust problem. They understood what you were offering, they might have the need, and they do not yet have enough reason to believe you specifically. This is the group people find most discouraging and the group where patient follow-up produces the highest return.

Three of those four are not rejections. They are unfinished conversations. But you can only treat them that way if you captured enough on the first call to know which category you are in.

The Real Job of the First Call

This is the reframe at the center of The 7 Minute Phone Call. The purpose of a first call is not to close. It is to learn enough to know what happens next, and to leave the other person willing to take your call again.

Those two objectives are compatible with a no. They are not compatible with pressure. When you push hard on a call where the timing is wrong, you convert a timing problem into a trust problem, and you burn a contact who would have been receptive in four months. The seven minute structure exists partly to prevent that. It gives you a defined length, which removes the pressure to keep talking past the point where the conversation has produced its useful information.

What you want off a call that does not convert is straightforward. What is their actual situation. What would need to change for this to become relevant. When does that change plausibly happen. Who else would be part of that decision. Four answers. If you have them, you have a follow-up plan. If you do not, all you have is a tally mark.

Building the System

Follow-up fails for almost everyone for the same reason: it depends on memory and motivation, and both run out. The fix is to make it a process rather than an intention.

Start with a single place where every conversation gets logged the same way. Not a notebook, not your inbox, not your head. One list with one structure, where every contact carries the same handful of fields: what they said, which of the four categories they fell into, the date the next touch is due, and what that touch should reference.

That last field matters more than people expect. A follow-up that says "just checking in" announces that you remember nothing about the last conversation and are contacting them because it is your turn, not because anything changed. A follow-up that says "you mentioned your lease runs through September and you did not want to make a change before then" proves you listened. The first one is noise. The second one is a continuation.

Then set the cadence by category. Timing problems get scheduled to the timeline the prospect gave you, plus a small buffer. Authority problems get a different approach entirely, because the next move is reaching a different person rather than re-contacting the same one. Trust problems get spaced, low-pressure contact over a longer horizon, ideally where you send something useful rather than ask for something. Genuine mismatches get removed, and removing them honestly is what makes the rest of the list worth working.

The discipline here is not glamorous. It is mostly the willingness to write four sentences immediately after a call that did not go well, at the exact moment you would rather move on to the next one. That is the whole habit. Everything downstream depends on it.

Why This Compounds and Cold Calling Does Not

A cold list is a depreciating asset. You work it once, you get whatever you get, and you need a new one. A well-maintained follow-up list is the opposite. Every call you make adds to it, so the list gets more valuable the longer you work, and the conversations get progressively warmer because the people on it have talked to you before.

After a year of doing this properly, a meaningful share of your pipeline comes from contacts you first spoke to eight or ten months earlier. Those conversations convert at a much higher rate, because the relationship has history and because you are reaching them at a moment you knew in advance would be relevant.

This is also why prospecting and operations are less separate than they look. A follow-up system is a system, in exactly the sense I write about in Built to Run. It is documented, it runs on a schedule, and it does not depend on any one person feeling motivated on a given Tuesday. Built correctly, it also transfers, which means the next person who joins your team inherits a working process rather than starting from zero.

Start Small

If you want to test this without rebuilding anything, take the last thirty prospecting conversations you can reconstruct. Sort them into the four categories. Throw out the genuine mismatches. For everything left, write the one sentence you would open with if you called them again, and put a date on it.

Most people who do this exercise find between eight and twelve contacts they had written off who were never actually a no. That is the point. The calls were fine. The system for what happens after them was missing.
